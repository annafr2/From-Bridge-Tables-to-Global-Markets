\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Background and Motivation}

Decision making under incomplete information is one of the hardest problems in artificial intelligence. While modern AI has reached super-human performance in fully observable games such as chess \citep{silver2017alphazero}, Go, and Atari \citep{schrittwieser2019muzero}, real-world strategic environments -- business negotiation in particular -- are characterized by hidden information, partial communication, and a mix of cooperation and competition that is hard to model directly.

\textbf{Bridge} is a unique testbed for these challenges. Each of the four players sees only 13 of the 52 cards, must cooperate with a partner while competing against two opponents, and communicates through a highly structured bidding language with approximately $10^{47}$ possible sequences. Bridge bidding is, formally, a multi-agent negotiation under imperfect information with measurable outcomes. \citet{rong2019competitive} showed that a deep learning system splitting bidding into estimation (ENN) and policy (PNN) networks can beat the world-leading rule-based engine Wbridge5; \citet{brown2019pluribus} demonstrated that imperfect-information AI can defeat elite humans in multi-player poker.

\textbf{NegoPlay} asks a more applied question: \emph{can we extract decision-making profiles from a large dataset of elite bridge play, and reuse those profiles to predict and simulate behavior in business negotiation scenarios?}

\section{Why this matters}

Business negotiation suffers from a fundamental data scarcity problem. Real negotiation transcripts are proprietary, outcomes are rarely measurable in standardized form, and no public datasets allow training of behavioral models. Bridge, in contrast, offers a clean laboratory:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item More than 149{,}000 structured decision points with measurable outcomes.
    \item Clear win/loss signals (contract success, IMP scores, VP conversion).
    \item Tournament-grade decisions by expert players, with explicit player identity per seat.
\end{itemize}

If profiles discovered in this clean environment transfer measurably to the noisier domain of negotiation, this opens a methodological path for studying business strategy without proprietary negotiation data.

\section{Literature Review and Positioning}
\label{sec:litreview}

This section grounds NegoPlay in the wider research conversation. Following \citet{lockett2007evolving}, we treat an unknown counterpart as a mixture of \emph{cardinal strategies} that can be discovered and re-used. We organize the literature into five themes.

\subsection*{Theme 1: Player-style clustering from gameplay sequences}

Our methodological anchor is \citet{talwadker2022cognitionnet}, who introduced \textbf{CognitionNet}. Using a sequence-to-sequence interpreter coupled to a classifier, they discovered behavioral profiles directly from raw Rummy telemetry. NegoPlay replaces ``Rummy telemetry'' with ``bridge bidding sequences'' but keeps the same logic: \emph{styles emerge from sequences of actions, without predefined rules.} \citet{sztyber2023bridgehand} provide an alternative path -- learning a continuous 8-dimensional embedding of bridge hands -- that we may incorporate in a second iteration.

\subsection*{Theme 2: Inference + decision split (theory of mind)}

Our second anchor is \citet{rong2019competitive}, whose ENN/PNN architecture separates the inference of partner's hidden cards from the action policy. \citet{wang2024inference} extend this with a conditional GAN inference module. NegoPlay applies the same split at the LLM level: the cluster profile is the \emph{inference} (``what kind of agent am I facing?''), and the system prompt is the \emph{policy} (``how do I act?''). This theory-of-mind-before-action structure is now standard in published bridge AI.

\subsection*{Theme 3: Opponent as mixture of cardinal styles}

\citet{lockett2007evolving} is the theoretical foundation for the entire project. Their result -- that explicit mixture coefficients over a small set of cardinal opponent types outperform monolithic networks -- justifies the central NegoPlay premise: every negotiation counterpart can be treated as a weighted mixture of the few profiles discovered in the bridge data.

\subsection*{Theme 4: Bridging structured games and natural-language negotiation}

\citet{james2021attitude} encode both actions and ``cheap talk'' messages into unified attitude vectors, then cluster them. This is the conceptual glue between the bridge stage (structured signals) and the negotiation stage (free-form text), and it is the paper we cite when defending the transfer claim.

\subsection*{Theme 5: Existing bridge AI engines as benchmarks}

\citet{yeh2018automatic} and \citet{kita2024simple} train deep RL bidders that beat Wbridge5; the open-source BEN engine \citep{lorserker_ben} provides a neural-network-based, scriptable opponent that we can use to ground LLM-vs-LLM games against an objective player. \citet{jarosz2022review} provides a clean survey of the field.

\subsection*{What others did, and what NegoPlay does differently}

\chapter{Research Question and Objectives}
\label{ch:rq}

\section{Primary Research Question}

\begin{quote}
\itshape Can LLM agents built from automatically-discovered bridge decision-making profiles exhibit behavioral consistency (\emph{Spearman} $\rho \geq 0.7$) between winning in bridge bidding simulations and winning in parallel business negotiation simulations?
\end{quote}

\section{Hypotheses}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2cm]
    \item[H1 -- Statistical:] K-means clustering on five behavioral features will produce 3--5 stable profiles with Silhouette score $\geq 0.4$ and $p < 0.05$ against random clustering.
    \item[H2 -- Behavioral:] LLM agents instantiated with these profiles will exhibit measurably different behaviors in bridge bidding (per-profile win-rate variance $> 15\%$).
    \item[H3 -- Transfer (main):] The win-rate ranking across profiles will correlate $\rho \geq 0.7$ (Spearman) between the bridge and negotiation domains.
    \item[Null:] No correlation $> 0.5$ between domains. This too is a valid research finding.
\end{description}

\section{Objectives}

The project has four operational objectives:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Statistical:} discover 3--5 stable decision profiles from $\approx$\,1{,}500 elite players.
    \item \textbf{Linguistic:} translate each profile into a 5--7 trait skill description using an LLM.
    \item \textbf{Engineering:} build four LLM agents (one per profile) with verifiable, structured output.
    \item \textbf{Empirical:} run dual simulations (bridge + negotiation) and measure cross-domain alignment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Anchor papers}

Following Dr.\ Yoram Segal's methodology framework, every project declares its anchor papers explicitly:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Anchor 1 (Stage 1 -- clustering):} \citet{talwadker2022cognitionnet}.
    \item \textbf{Anchor 2 (Stage 3 -- inference/decision split):} \citet{rong2019competitive}.
    \item \textbf{Theoretical foundation:} \citet{lockett2007evolving}.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Why two thresholds appear.} The legacy threshold of 20 boards was used in
the v2.0 Stage~1 pipeline (April 2026), which produced 64 ``Slam Hunters''
out of 1{,}572 qualifying players. PhD supervisor Dr.\ Nezer Jacob Zaidenberg
identified a sample-size problem: with only 20 boards and a rare-event rate
near 5\,\%, the 95\,\% confidence interval is too wide to distinguish a true
Slam Hunter from random variation. The pipeline was revised to require
$\geq 50$ boards on \emph{both} the declared and the bidding axis, plus a
one-sided binomial test (Eq.~\eqref{eq:binomial}). The cohort that enters
all numerical results in this report is therefore the \textbf{563-player}
v2.1 cohort; the 1{,}572 figure is shown only as a reference for the
underlying data scale. The full revision is documented in
RESEARCH\_INSIGHTS.md, sections Q7.5--Q7.6.

\section{Stage 1 -- Profile Discovery (ML)}

\textbf{Ten} behavioural features are computed per player and partitioned into two groups: \emph{outcome features} extracted from the \texttt{contract} column (any board the player declared) and \emph{process features} extracted from the \texttt{bidding} column (any board where the auction is recorded, regardless of who declared). The process / outcome split follows the methodological anchor of \citet{rong2019competitive}, who separate the inference (auction-process) signal from the action (outcome) signal.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Outcome features (declarer only, 5):}
      \texttt{slam\_rate} (\%\ of declarations at slam level 6--7);
      \texttt{double\_rate} (\%\ of declared contracts that opponents doubled);
      \texttt{avg\_level} (mean contract level $\in [1,7]$);
      \texttt{nt\_rate} (\%\ of declarations in NoTrump);
      \texttt{partscore\_rate} (\%\ of declarations at level 1--3).
    \item \textbf{Process features (any seat, from full bidding, 5):}
      \texttt{opening\_rate} (\%\ of boards where this player opened the auction);
      \texttt{preempt\_rate} (\%\ of opening bids at level $\geq 2$);
      \texttt{intervention\_rate} (\%\ of boards with a competitive bid into the opponents' auction);
      \texttt{penalty\_double\_rate} (\%\ of boards with a doubling call by this player);
      \texttt{avg\_bids\_per\_board} (mean number of bids per board).
\end{itemize}

\textit{Note on legacy features.} An earlier draft used a 5-feature set including \texttt{success\_rate} and a composite \texttt{avg\_risk\_score}; both were dropped after the variance audit in Chapter~\ref{ch:tech} showed that \texttt{success\_rate} provides almost no separating signal at elite level (all elite players succeed at $\approx 80\,\%$) and that \texttt{avg\_risk\_score} is a linear combination of other features and adds no new information.

After variance and correlation filtering (Chapter~\ref{ch:tech}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv}), the 10 features reduce to a stable 7--8 axis behaviour space depending on the configuration (8 for the production K-Means / extreme-percentile pipeline; 7 for the V3 audit configuration which also drops \texttt{partscore\_rate}). Features are then standardised via \texttt{StandardScaler} (Eq.~\eqref{eq:zscore}), reduced via PCA (Eq.~\eqref{eq:pca_eig}), and assigned to one of five profiles via the extreme-percentile + binomial-test rule (Eq.~\eqref{eq:binomial}).

\section{Stage 2 -- Skill Extraction (LLM)}

For each of the four extreme profiles we sampled five players and sent their
actual played boards to \textbf{Gemini~2.5~Flash} in chunks of up to 25 boards,
with a strict JSON schema. Each call returns 5--7 decision-making skills, each
with a confidence label and supporting board references. A profile-specific
guidance prompt (\texttt{PROFILE\_GUIDANCE}) requires at least two skills to lie
on the profile's defining axis; without it, an unguided prompt returned the
generic ``aggressive competitive bidding'' for every profile.

\subsection*{Semantic aggregation}
Raw skill names vary in wording (e.g.\ ``initiates slam exploration with
splinters'' vs.\ ``employs splinter bids for slam exploration''). We merge
synonymous skills with TF--IDF cosine similarity and Union--Find clustering
(merge threshold $0.40$; the lower value $0.30$ produced false merges such as
``control bidding'' with ``competitive overcalling''). The most frequent raw
name in each cluster becomes the canonical skill label.

\subsection*{Empirical validation}
The central question is whether the LLM-described profiles are \emph{real}
behavioural types or an artefact of the clustering / an LLM hallucination. We
therefore measured each profile's defining behaviour directly from the raw
bidding and contract data---no LLM, no clustering---and compared it against a
Generalist baseline drawn from the same elite population (an
\emph{elite-vs-elite} comparison, which makes any separation conservative).
Denominators match Stage~1 exactly: declarer-only boards for the contract-level
metrics (slam, partscore, NT) and per-board-with-bidding for the Fighter's
penalty doubles. Effect size is Cohen's~$d$; significance is a one-sided
Mann--Whitney~$U$ test. All four profiles separate from the baseline with large
effect sizes and statistical significance (Table~\ref{tab:stage2validation}).

the verdict ``proceed to Stage~3''. The human-readable profile names are
\emph{Slam Hunter}, \emph{Insurance Player}, \emph{Fighter}, and
\emph{NT Specialist}.

\subsection*{Methodological note}
The Fighter metric was initially measured per call (doubles $\div$ total calls),
which gave a weak, overlapping signal and was rejected on expert review.
Switching to the Stage-1-aligned per-board metric (boards with at least one
double $\div$ boards with bidding) produced the clean $\times1.31$ separation
above. The validation denominator must match the clustering denominator
exactly, or a genuine profile can appear spurious.

\section{Stage 3 -- Agent Construction}

Each profile becomes an LLM agent through a structured system prompt that contains: identity (profile name), 5--7 traits, domain context (bridge \textit{or} negotiation), output schema (JSON), and 1--2 few-shot examples. Two agent classes are implemented:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \texttt{BridgeAgent.make\_bid(hand, auction)} -- returns a valid bridge bid.
    \item \texttt{NegotiationAgent.respond\_to\_offer(scenario, history, offer)} -- returns a counter-offer plus reasoning.
\end{itemize}

\section{Stage 4 -- Dual Simulation and Alignment}

\textbf{4a Bridge:} 50 randomly generated hands are played by combinations of profile agents; results are scored in IMPs. As a non-LLM benchmark, profile agents can also play one side of a board against the open-source BEN engine \citep{lorserker_ben} sitting on the other side.

\textbf{4b Negotiation:} four scenarios (M\&A, JV equity split, B2B SaaS pricing, government tender) are run for each pair of agents, with up to 10 rounds and an explicit walk-away condition.

\textbf{4c Alignment:} per-profile win rates are computed for both domains; the cross-domain alignment is measured by \emph{Spearman} correlation. A scatter plot is produced; significance is tested via a two-sided permutation test.

\subsection{Notation}

Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ denote the player-feature matrix where $n = 563$ is the number of qualifying players (those with $\geq 50$ declared boards \emph{and} $\geq 50$ bidding boards). The $i$-th row $\mathbf{x}_i$ represents one player; the $j$-th column $\mathbf{X}_{\cdot j}$ represents one feature.

\textbf{Two configurations of $p$.} The feature count depends on the pipeline:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Production pipeline ($p = 8$):} we start from the 10 features of Section~\ref{ch:method}, then drop \texttt{avg\_level} ($\mathrm{CV} = 0.04$) and \texttt{avg\_bids\_per\_board} ($\mathrm{CV} = 0.08$) for failing the variance gate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv}.
    \item \textbf{V3 audit configuration ($p = 7$):} same as production, but \texttt{partscore\_rate} ($\mathrm{CV} = 0.09$) is also dropped as borderline-low-variance during Dr.\ Rami's preprocessing audit. The Mahalanobis-distance outlier detection below is run in the V3 configuration, hence $p = 7$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mahalanobis_threshold}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Z-score standardisation}

To make features comparable across different scales (e.g., \texttt{slam\_rate} $\in [0, 0.15]$ vs.\ \texttt{nt\_rate} $\in [0, 0.45]$), each feature is standardised to zero mean and unit variance:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zscore}
z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \mu_j}{\sigma_j}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$z_{ij}$] standardised score of player $i$ on feature $j$.
    \item[$x_{ij}$] raw value of feature $j$ for player $i$ (e.g., player's observed \texttt{slam\_rate}).
    \item[$\mu_j$] population mean of feature $j$ across all $n = 563$ qualifying players: $\mu_j = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{ij}$.
    \item[$\sigma_j$] population standard deviation of feature $j$: $\sigma_j = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{ij} - \mu_j)^2}$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Equation~\eqref{eq:zscore} is implemented by \texttt{sklearn.preprocessing.StandardScaler}. A $z$-score of $+2$ means the player is two population standard deviations above the mean on that feature; in a normal distribution this corresponds to the $\approx$\,97.5\textsuperscript{th} percentile. We use the same formula both for preprocessing (input to K-Means / PCA) and for profile-significance scoring (column \texttt{profile\_z} in \texttt{player\_profiles.csv}).

\subsection{Variance audit (coefficient of variation)}

Following Dr.\ Rami's preprocessing review, we audit each feature by its coefficient of variation:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cv}
\mathrm{CV}_j = \frac{\sigma_j}{|\mu_j|}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$\mathrm{CV}_j$] coefficient of variation of feature $j$ (dimensionless).
    \item[$\sigma_j$] population standard deviation of feature $j$ (as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:zscore}).
    \item[$\mu_j$] population mean of feature $j$ (as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:zscore}).
\end{itemize}

\noindent Features with $\mathrm{CV}_j < 0.10$ are discarded as low-information: their relative spread is below 10\% of the mean, so they cannot meaningfully distinguish players. In our data, \texttt{avg\_level} ($\mathrm{CV} = 0.04$) and \texttt{avg\_bids\_per\_board} ($\mathrm{CV} = 0.08$) were removed by this filter.

\subsection{Principal Component Analysis}

PCA computes the eigendecomposition of the standardised covariance matrix:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pca_cov}
\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{n-1}\, \mathbf{Z}^\top \mathbf{Z}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$\mathbf{C}$] $p \times p$ covariance matrix of the standardised features.
    \item[$\mathbf{Z}$] $n \times p$ matrix of $z$-scores from Eq.~\eqref{eq:zscore} ($n = 563$, $p = 8$).
    \item[$n-1$] degrees-of-freedom correction (Bessel's correction).
\end{itemize}

Solving the eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pca_eig}
\mathbf{C}\, \mathbf{v}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{v}_k, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, p
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$\mathbf{v}_k$] $k$-th eigenvector (principal direction in feature space).
    \item[$\lambda_k$] $k$-th eigenvalue, ordered $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_p$.
    \item[$k$] component index, $k \in \{1, \ldots, p\}$.
\end{itemize}

The fraction of variance explained by component $k$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pca_var}
\mathrm{EV}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_{i=1}^{p} \lambda_i}
\end{equation}

We retain the top $K$ components that satisfy $\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathrm{EV}_k \geq 0.80$ (cumulative variance threshold). In our data, $K = 5$ components achieve 85.2\% cumulative variance: $\mathrm{EV} = (0.246, 0.191, 0.135, 0.103, 0.077)$.

\subsection{Clustering validity: Silhouette score}

For each player $i$ assigned to cluster $C_{k(i)}$, define:
\begin{align}
a(i) &= \frac{1}{|C_{k(i)}| - 1} \sum_{\substack{j \in C_{k(i)} \\ j \neq i}} d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \label{eq:silhouette_a} \\
b(i) &= \min_{\ell \neq k(i)} \; \frac{1}{|C_\ell|} \sum_{j \in C_\ell} d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \label{eq:silhouette_b}
\end{align}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$a(i)$] mean Euclidean distance from player $i$ to all \emph{other} players in their own cluster $C_{k(i)}$ -- the ``cohesion'' term.
    \item[$b(i)$] mean Euclidean distance from player $i$ to the nearest \emph{other} cluster -- the ``separation'' term. The min ranges over all clusters $\ell \neq k(i)$.
    \item[$d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$] Euclidean distance in the scaled PCA space.
    \item[$C_{k(i)}$] the cluster to which player $i$ is assigned.
\end{itemize}

The silhouette score for player $i$ and the overall mean are:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:silhouette}
s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\{a(i), b(i)\}}, \qquad \bar{s} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} s(i)
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Range:} $s(i) \in [-1, +1]$ for every player. Interpretation:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$\bar{s} \geq 0.50$] strong cluster structure.
    \item[$0.25 \leq \bar{s} < 0.50$] weak structure.
    \item[$\bar{s} < 0.25$] no meaningful cluster structure.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Our maximum across five preprocessing configurations was $\bar{s} = 0.24$ (V2: 8 features + PCA(3), Z-score). Computed via \texttt{sklearn.metrics.silhouette\_score}.

\subsection{Mahalanobis-distance outlier detection}

For multivariate outlier detection prior to clustering (V3 configuration only), we use the squared Mahalanobis distance:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mahalanobis}
D^2(\mathbf{x}_i) = (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\, \mathbf{S}^{-1}\, (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu})
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$D^2(\mathbf{x}_i)$] squared Mahalanobis distance of player $i$ from the data centre.
    \item[$\mathbf{x}_i$] feature vector of player $i$ (after scaling).
    \item[$\boldsymbol{\mu}$] sample mean vector of all players, $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^p$.
    \item[$\mathbf{S}^{-1}$] inverse of the empirical covariance matrix (estimated by \texttt{sklearn.covariance.EmpiricalCovariance}). The inverse weights features by their inter-correlations, so a player who is unusual on \emph{correlated} features counts as more outlying.
\end{itemize}

Under multivariate normality, $D^2 \sim \chi^2_p$, so player $i$ is flagged as an outlier when

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mahalanobis_threshold}
D^2(\mathbf{x}_i) > \chi^2_{p,\, 1 - \alpha}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$\chi^2_{p, 1-\alpha}$] $(1-\alpha)$ critical value of the chi-square distribution with $p$ degrees of freedom (number of features). Computed via \texttt{scipy.stats.chi2.ppf(1-$\alpha$, df=$p$)}.
    \item[$\alpha$] significance level; we use $\alpha = 0.01$ (top 1\% of the tail). For $p = 7$, this gives a threshold of $\approx 18.5$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\emph{Methodological note:} outlier removal is appropriate for K-Means and GMM but counterproductive for the extreme-percentile method, since the outliers are precisely the profile members we want to identify.

\subsection{Extreme-percentile profile assignment}

The production pipeline uses an extreme-percentile method on the standardised features, supported by a binomial significance test. For each profile axis $j$ (slam\_rate, partscore\_rate, nt\_rate, penalty\_double\_rate), we:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Identify the top 10\% of players on $z_{\cdot j}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:zscore}).
    \item Test the null hypothesis that their observed rate equals the population baseline $p_0$ using a one-sided binomial test (Eq.~\ref{eq:binomial}).
    \item Assign the player to profile $j$ only if both gates pass.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:binomial}
\mathrm{p\text{-value}}_i = \sum_{m = k_i}^{n_i} \binom{n_i}{m} p_0^{\,m}\, (1 - p_0)^{n_i - m}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$n_i$] player $i$'s denominator: \texttt{n\_declared} for outcome features (Slam Hunter, Insurance Player, NT Specialist) or \texttt{n\_bidding\_boards} for process features (Fighter).
    \item[$k_i$] number of ``successes'' for player $i$ on the profile axis (e.g., number of slam contracts declared, for Slam Hunter).
    \item[$p_0$] population baseline rate of the event (e.g., $p_0 = 0.055$ for slam).
    \item[$\binom{n_i}{m}$] binomial coefficient ``$n_i$ choose $m$''.
    \item[$m$] summation index over all outcomes at least as extreme as the observed $k_i$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation:} the p-value is the probability of observing at least $k_i$ successes under the null hypothesis that the player's true rate equals the baseline $p_0$ (one-sided, ``greater than'' alternative). We require $\mathrm{p\text{-value}}_i < 0.05$ for profile assignment. Implemented as \texttt{scipy.stats.binomtest($k_i$, $n_i$, $p_0$, alternative=`greater')}.

\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Temperature sampling}

LLM token selection follows a softmax over logits, modulated by a temperature parameter $T$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:softmax_temperature}
P(\text{token} = t \mid \text{context}) = \frac{\exp(z_t / T)}{\sum_{t' = 1}^{V} \exp(z_{t'} / T)}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$P(t \mid \text{ctx})$] probability that the model emits token $t$ next, given context.
    \item[$z_t$] raw logit of token $t$ produced by the model's final linear layer.
    \item[$V$] vocabulary size (Gemini's tokeniser: $\approx 256\text{K}$ tokens).
    \item[$T$] temperature parameter, $T > 0$. As $T \to 0$ the distribution becomes peaked (deterministic, argmax); as $T \to \infty$ it becomes uniform.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Choices for NegoPlay:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item $T = 0.3$ for bridge bidding (consistency is critical: the same hand should produce the same bid).
    \item $T = 0.7$ for negotiation (diversity of offers is part of the experiment).
    \item $T = 0.1$ for the Bridge-Expert Validation skill (we want deterministic expert opinions).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Per-call cost accounting}

Each LLM call writes a JSON-Lines record to \texttt{results/llm\_logs/}. The cost is computed as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:llm_cost}
C_{\text{call}} = \frac{T_{\text{in}} \cdot c_{\text{in}} + T_{\text{out}} \cdot c_{\text{out}}}{10^6}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$C_{\text{call}}$] cost of a single LLM call, in US dollars.
    \item[$T_{\text{in}}$] number of input tokens (system prompt + user message), reported by the provider's API metadata.
    \item[$T_{\text{out}}$] number of output tokens (completion).
    \item[$c_{\text{in}}$] unit cost per $10^6$ input tokens (\$0.075 for Gemini Flash 2.0).
    \item[$c_{\text{out}}$] unit cost per $10^6$ output tokens (\$0.30 for Gemini Flash 2.0).
\end{itemize}

\noindent The cumulative cost is monitored against a hard cap of \$50, with an alert at \$20.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:winrate}
w_k^D = \frac{1}{N_D} \sum_{g = 1}^{N_D} \mathbf{1}[\text{agent}_{P_k} \text{ won game } g]
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$w_k^D$] win-rate of profile $k$ in domain $D \in \{\text{bridge}, \text{negotiation}\}$, $w_k^D \in [0, 1]$.
    \item[$N_D$] number of games in domain $D$ ($N_{\text{bridge}} = N_{\text{negotiation}} = 50$ in Phase~1).
    \item[{$\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$}] indicator function: $1$ if the inner condition is true, $0$ otherwise.
    \item[$P_k$] one of the four behavioural profiles ($k \in \{1, \ldots, 4\}$), excluding the Generalist baseline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Spearman rank correlation}

The main outcome measure of NegoPlay. Let $r_k^{br}$ and $r_k^{ng}$ be the ranks of profile $k$ within its domain (rank $1$ = highest win-rate). The Spearman correlation, when there are no ties, is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spearman}
\rho_s = 1 - \frac{6 \sum_{k=1}^{K} d_k^{\,2}}{K(K^2 - 1)}, \qquad d_k = r_k^{br} - r_k^{ng}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$\rho_s$] Spearman rank correlation, $\rho_s \in [-1, +1]$. Value $+1$ means perfect rank agreement; $-1$ means perfect rank inversion; $0$ means no correlation.
    \item[$K$] number of profiles compared ($K = 4$).
    \item[$d_k$] difference in ranks of profile $k$ between bridge and negotiation.
\end{itemize}

The general formula (which handles ties correctly) is the Pearson correlation applied to the ranks:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spearman_general}
\rho_s = \frac{\sum_k (r_k^{br} - \bar{r}^{br})(r_k^{ng} - \bar{r}^{ng})}{\sqrt{\sum_k (r_k^{br} - \bar{r}^{br})^2\, \sum_k (r_k^{ng} - \bar{r}^{ng})^2}}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$r_k^{br}, r_k^{ng}$] ranks of profile $k$ in bridge and negotiation respectively.
    \item[$\bar{r}^{br}, \bar{r}^{ng}$] mean ranks (always $(K+1)/2 = 2.5$ for $K=4$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Decision rule:} $\rho_s \geq 0.70 \Rightarrow$ support for H3 (alignment hypothesis); $0.50 \leq \rho_s < 0.70 \Rightarrow$ partial support; $\rho_s < 0.50 \Rightarrow$ null result. Significance is tested by a two-sided permutation test with $10{,}000$ random permutations of the negotiation win-rate vector. Implemented as \texttt{scipy.stats.spearmanr}.

\subsection{Falsifiability test (Phase 3)}

To rule out the \emph{tautological alignment} confound (\textit{i.e.}, the LLM merely following the prompt rather than transferring a learned style), we run an inverse-prompt control:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:falsify}
\rho_{\text{inverse}} = \mathrm{Spearman}\bigl(\{w_k^{br}\}_{k=1}^{K},\ \{w_k^{ng,\text{inverse}}\}_{k=1}^{K}\bigr)
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.6cm, itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item[$w_k^{ng,\text{inverse}}$] negotiation win-rate of an agent prompted with the \emph{opposite} profile (e.g., a Slam Hunter in bridge becomes an Insurance-style agent in negotiation).
    \item[$\rho_{\text{inverse}}$] alignment under the inverted-prompt control.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Falsifiability criterion:} if $\rho_{\text{inverse}} \geq 0.70$, the original alignment is a prompt-following artefact, not a true behavioural transfer, and H3 must be rejected even when the main $\rho_s$ is above threshold.

\chapter{Summary}
\label{ch:summary}

\section{What this report covers}

This preliminary report defines NegoPlay -- a five-week project to discover decision-making profiles from 149{,}208 elite bridge tournament hands and to test, via LLM agents, whether the same profiles predict behavior in business negotiation simulations. It anchors the work in two published papers (\citealp{talwadker2022cognitionnet} for clustering; \citealp{rong2019competitive} for the inference/decision split) and a theoretical foundation (\citealp{lockett2007evolving}). It specifies the dataset, the four-stage methodology, the seven work packages, the WP\,$\times$\,session matrix, the critical path, the deliverables, and ten concrete project risks with mitigations.

\section{Expected contribution}

If the cross-domain alignment hypothesis is supported ($\rho \geq 0.7$), NegoPlay will be the first publicly reproducible demonstration that strategic profiles discovered in a clean game-theoretic environment (bridge) measurably predict behavior in a different, less structured domain (business negotiation). This contributes to the long-running PhD research thread \emph{From Bridge Tables to Global Markets} at LUT University.

If the hypothesis is not supported, the project still contributes: (i) a validated set of bridge decision profiles, useful in its own right for bridge analytics and coaching; (ii) a unified, provider-neutral LLM client; and (iii) a clean methodological template for cross-domain behavioral transfer studies.

\section{Beyond the course: link to the PhD}

NegoPlay is the empirical baseline for Paper 1 of the author's PhD thesis. The four discovered profiles, the prompt library and the alignment methodology are designed to be reused in PhD Year 2 with: a larger dataset (BBO Vugraph adds millions of deals), a deeper model (the ENN+PNN+PPO stack described by \citealp{rong2019competitive} and \citealp{kita2024simple}), and real negotiation transcripts (Year 2--3). This continuity between the course project and the PhD is intentional: the same single-SDK code base will carry the work forward.

\section{Stage 4 -- Simulation protocol}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Bridge games:} 50 randomly generated PBN deals per matchup. Each agent receives only its own 13 cards. The simulator records the full auction, final contract, declarer, tricks, and IMP score relative to a fixed datum table.
    \item \textbf{Negotiation games:} four scenarios (M\&A, JV equity, B2B SaaS pricing, government tender), each run 12--13 times per matchup for 50 games total. Maximum 10 rounds per scenario; explicit walk-away condition logged.
    \item \textbf{Logging:} every game writes a structured record to \texttt{results/} including provider, model, full prompt, full response, tokens, latency, cost, and outcome.
    \item \textbf{Random seeds:} the deal generator and the negotiation initial-state seed are derived from a master seed of 42, fixed in \texttt{src/sdk.py}.
\end{itemize}

\section{How to reproduce, end-to-end}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \texttt{git clone https://github.com/annafr2/bridge-business-research.git}
    \item \texttt{cd bridge-business-research/projects/negoplay}
    \item \texttt{python3.12 -m venv .venv \&\& source .venv/bin/activate}
    \item \texttt{pip install -r requirements.txt}
    \item \texttt{cp .env.example .env} -- add your \texttt{GOOGLE\_API\_KEY}.
    \item \texttt{python -m src.stage1\_clustering} -- regenerates \texttt{player\_profiles.csv} (ML only, no API cost).
    \item \texttt{python notebooks/visualize\_profiles.py} -- regenerates all 5 figures in \texttt{docs/images/}.
    \item \texttt{python notebooks/preprocessing\_comparison.py} -- reruns the 5-configuration preprocessing audit.
    \item \texttt{python -m src.stage2\_skills} -- runs Gemini skill extraction (Stage 2 onward; incurs API cost $\sim$\$0.05).
    \item \texttt{python -m src.stage4\_simulate -{}-bridge-games 50 -{}-negotiation-games 50}.
    \item \texttt{python -m src.report} -- produces \texttt{results/alignment\_report.md}.
\end{enumerate}
